ECTRIMS[^ectrims] 2026 had its patient community day this past Friday. What follows are the main bullet points of what was discussed in the online stream.

## Part 1: MS, NMOSD, and MOGAD clinical updates

### Welcome and Opening Remarks

- First Patient Community Day in North America, held in Toronto as a joint ECTRIMS/ACTRIMS event
- Over 4,000 registrations: record attendance, both in-person and online
- Key theme: bridging patient lived experience with research and clinical expertise
- Every session featured people with lived experience on stage asking panelist questions
- First year with a dedicated NMOSD[^nmosd]/MOGAD[^mogad] session and a closing 1-hour livestream Q&A

### CAR-T Cell Therapy

- Experimental therapy[^cart] borrowed from oncology, currently accessible only via clinical trials
- Three delivery approaches:
    - Autologous: patient’s own cells extracted, engineered, reinfused
    - Allogeneic (“off the shelf”): donor cells engineered and given to patient
    - In vivo: proteins administered to engineer cells inside the body directly
- Key advantage: penetrates the CNS[^cns], reaching cells hidden in the brain that conventional drugs cannot
- Potential dual benefit in MS:
    - Eliminates relapse-driving cells while allowing new healthy cells to regenerate
    - May impact progressive MS mechanisms via CNS penetration
- Known risks: inflammatory toxicity, requirement for pre-treatment chemotherapy
- “Basket studies”[^basket] now enrolling patients with MS, NMOSD, myasthenia gravis, and demyelinating neuropathies in a single trial design

### BTK Inhibitors

- ~7-8 BTK inhibitors[^btk] under study in MS, each with slightly different drug properties
- Mechanism: inhibits BTK molecule in B cells and myeloid cells[^bcells], reducing activation both peripherally and in the CNS
- Trial updates presented at MS Toronto 2026:
    - [Tolebrutinib](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=34): positive in secondary progressive MS[^phenotypes]; approved in Europe, uncertain US status after FDA issues
    - [Fenibrutinib](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=24): positive in relapsing MS and non-inferior[^noninferior] to [ocrelizumab](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=6) in primary progressive MS
        - New MRI[^mri] data: reduced slowly expanding lesions (SELs) and paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs)[^sels]
    - [Remibrutinib](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=136): clearly reduced relapses and new MRI lesions vs. [teriflunomide](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=37)
        - Disability endpoints showed trend toward benefit but did not reach statistical significance[^endpoints]
        - Notably, no liver safety signal observed (a concern with earlier BTK inhibitors)
- Liver toxicity has been a class-wide concern; remibrutinib data is encouraging on this front

### Treatment De-escalation and Discontinuation

- Growing evidence base on when and how to safely reduce or stop MS treatment
- De-escalation: starting high-efficacy[^efficacy], stepping down; or spacing out high-efficacy dosing to reduce side-effect risk
- Stopping treatment:
    - Not recommended in younger patients on moderate-efficacy drugs: relapse risk returns
    - May be appropriate in older patients on milder therapies, on a case-by-case basis
    - Aging immune system naturally reduces relapse frequency and new lesion formation
- Registry studies and randomized trials both contributing data
- AI and statistical modeling being explored to individualize discontinuation decisions

### Myelin Repair and EBV Prevention

- Myelin repair (remyelination)[^myelin]:
    - Oligodendrocytes[^oligo] are the target cells; many compounds identified in animal/lab models
    - 1-2 trials showing early positive biomarker[^biomarker] signals (electrophysiology, MRI velocity measures)
    - Challenge: no trial yet shows robust clinical recovery; trial design for repair endpoints still evolving
    - Significant industry interest; meaningful results expected within 3-4 years
- EBV[^ebv] and MS prevention:
    - EBV is required but not sufficient for MS development
    - Growing evidence EBV may also drive relapses and progressive mechanisms
    - Prevention trials are complex: large populations, long follow-up needed
    - Infectious mononucleosis[^mono] as an earlier, more tractable endpoint for vaccine trials
    - First-degree relatives (~3-5% MS risk) identified as a tractable high-risk trial population
    - Moderna studying an EBV vaccine in people already living with MS to prevent relapses

### NMOSD and MOGAD Updates

- NMOSD pre-clinical phase:
    - AQP4[^aqp4] antibodies detectable years before clinical onset
    - At that stage, antibodies present but no astrocyte damage yet
    - Significance of early antibody detection and whether to treat is an open question
- CAR-T in NMOSD: early Phase 1 data positive; multiple trials starting; Phase 3[^phases] potentially launchable in 2-3 years if results hold
- Relapse management in NMOSD:
    - Debate: methylprednisolone alone vs. adding plasma exchange vs. complement inhibitors[^relapse]
    - Panel view: combination of all anti-inflammatory approaches likely optimal to limit disability
    - Access gaps remain: plasma exchange not always available on weekends or at all centers
- MOGAD treatment advances:
    - METEORITE trial (satralizumab, anti-IL-6[^il6]): first proven preventive treatment, Phase 3
    - TOMATO trial (tocilizumab, anti-IL-6 receptor): positive results in Chinese cohort, more globally accessible
    - Both target the IL-6 pathway; mechanism validated across two agents
    - No approved treatments yet in the US; approvals anticipated as trials complete
    - MOGAD attacks tend to recur at the same anatomical site (optic nerve or spinal cord), raising disability risk with each relapse
- Treatment choice in MOGAD: shared decision-making on route (injection vs. infusion), frequency, and lifestyle fit; cross-trial comparison difficult due to different designs

### Outlook and Key Messages

- NMOSD: 3-4 approved therapies now available in many regions; 10 years ago there were none
- MOGAD: first preventive treatments on the horizon; multiple additional trials underway
- Research is accelerating by applying lessons from the MS treatment landscape
- Biomarkers (GFAP, NfL[^gfapnfl]) increasingly useful for monitoring disease activity in NMOSD/MOGAD
- Panelists emphasized gratitude to patients participating in trials: multi-day travel, repeated assessments, all voluntary

## Part 2: remyelination, aging, brain health, and lived experience

### Remyelination: Where the Science Stands

- Trials over the past 10 years confirm remyelination is achievable in humans
    - Signals seen in visual nerve conduction speed[^conduction] and MRI myelin content
    - No clear link to disability yet: trials run only 3-6 months, too short to show neuroprotection
- Benefit is greatest earlier in disease course
    - Fewer intact nerve fibers remain as MS progresses, reducing the magnitude of effect
    - Clearer results in relapsing MS vs. progressive MS so far
    - Still considered beneficial in progressive MS, just harder to demonstrate
- [Metformin](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=4) trial[^metformin] deployed to rejuvenate aged stem cells and extend remyelination benefit across age groups
- Remyelination agents may also function as senotherapeutics[^seno], slowing biological aging
    - [Chris McMurran](/authors/0000-0002-8710-0930/) (Cambridge) showed one repurposed drug slowed brain aging on MRI

### Aging, Biological Age, and MS

- Biological age[^bioage] diverges from chronological age in MS: disease appears to accelerate aging
    - Metabolic and other aging markers give a consistent picture of accelerated aging in some patients
- Disease course tends to shift around age 50-55: fewer relapses, less inflammatory MRI activity, but higher risk of progressive disability
- De-escalation or extended dosing of B-cell/infusion therapies is a valid strategy from ~50-55
    - Should be individualized based on biological age, not calendar age
- 60% of MS patients aged 60 in one Dutch cohort had undiagnosed high cholesterol
    - Highlights how comorbidities[^comorbid] accumulate unnoticed alongside MS
- Sit-to-stand tests proposed as better measures of physical aging than standard walking speed

### Hormones, Lifespan, and Pregnancy

- Lifespan milestones (puberty, perimenopause, pregnancy) all influence how MS expresses
    - Applies to men and boys too, not only women
- Perimenopause is a multi-year process, not a single event; increasingly recognized as influencing MS course
    - HRT[^hrt] data presented: at minimum safe, potentially beneficial for MS course and symptoms
- Females tend to have higher relapse rates from puberty to midlife; males show earlier progression risk
- Pregnancy data is now highly reassuring
    - Third trimester naturally suppresses relapses
    - Postpartum treatment (1-3 weeks after birth) now well-supported by data
    - Rates of pregnancy in MS are rising: a direct result of improved safety confidence

### MS Prodrome and Early Identification

- Prodrome = the period before first recognizable MS symptom onset
    - Up to 15 years before onset, patients more likely to visit physicians for ill-defined symptoms
    - Fatigue, IBS, restless legs, anxiety, and depression elevated ~10+ years before onset
    - Physical activity starts dropping ~15 years before onset, with a second drop ~4-5 years before
- Psychiatric prodromal features associated with greater disability progression after MS onset
- Field is not yet able to accurately identify pre-MS individuals, but a pathway is emerging
    - Goal: enroll prodromal individuals in remyelination or prevention trials

### Brain Health, Exercise, and Comorbidities

- WHO recommends 150 minutes of activity per week; literature supports ~2x1hr sessions for measurable benefit
    - Benefits seen in balance, muscle strength, and movement
    - Aerobic and resistance training can protect or even strengthen brain volume
- Exercise does not have to be intensive to count
    - Walking slightly faster than usual, or raising heart rate modestly, shows brain benefit
    - Dual-tasking (cognitive + motor) helps restore efficient brain connectivity
    - Breaking exercise into 10-15 minute chunks throughout the day is a valid strategy
- Cardiovascular disease is underdiagnosed and undertreated in MS, and linked to faster disability progression
- Comorbidities (anxiety, depression, thyroid disease, IBD, cardiovascular disease) frequently co-occur with MS and complicate disease management

### Lived Experience Panel: Key Themes

- Personalized medicine is the shared aspiration: one-size-fits-all treatment algorithms do not reflect real-world MS
    - “Average patient problem”: trial cohorts often skew younger, healthier, and whiter than the real MS population
- Shift toward patient-centric research is underway but needs to go further
    - People with lived experience should be embedded at the study design stage, not just recruited as participants
    - Societies and patient organizations can connect individuals to researchers
- Strong call to participate in clinical trials: the only way research advances is through patient participation
- [Stem cell](/conditions/multiple-sclerosis/research-papers/?category=9) therapy: replacing lost neurons is not yet achievable
    - Current focus: harnessing the brain’s own stem cells and making the environment more youthful
    - Preclinical[^preclinical] (animal) work by groups like Pluchino et al. shows myelin regeneration is possible
- Closing message from all three panelists: overwhelming hope
    - MS care today is unrecognizable from 15 years ago
    - Work is happening across remyelination, prevention, brain health, and quality of life simultaneously

## In Plain-English

[^ectrims]: ECTRIMS is the European Committee for Treatment and Research in Multiple Sclerosis, and ACTRIMS is its counterpart for the Americas. Both are societies of MS doctors and researchers, and the joint meeting is where new results are presented first.
[^nmosd]: Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder. A rare autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the optic nerves and spinal cord, and sometimes the brain. It is a different disease from MS and needs different treatment.
[^mogad]: MOG antibody-associated disease. A rare condition in which antibodies against a protein on myelin called MOG cause attacks on the optic nerves, spinal cord or brain. It can look like MS but is a separate disease with its own treatments.
[^cart]: CAR-T cell therapy. A person's own immune cells (T cells) are collected, modified in a lab so they hunt a specific target, and returned to the body. It was developed for blood cancers. In MS and related diseases the target is B cells, the immune cells that drive the attack.
[^cns]: The central nervous system: the brain, spinal cord and optic nerves. Many drugs struggle to get in because of the blood-brain barrier, a protective filter around the brain.
[^basket]: A basket study tests one treatment in several different diseases at once, grouping people by what the treatment targets rather than by their diagnosis.
[^btk]: Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) is an enzyme that B cells and some other immune cells use to switch on. BTK inhibitors are pills that block it. Some can enter the brain and spinal cord, which is why they are being studied, especially for progressive MS.
[^bcells]: Two kinds of immune cell. B cells make antibodies and help trigger attacks on myelin. Myeloid cells, which include the brain's own immune cells (microglia), are involved in the slow, ongoing inflammation inside the brain and spinal cord.
[^phenotypes]: Relapsing MS means attacks (relapses) followed by recovery. Secondary progressive MS is a later phase where disability slowly builds up, with or without relapses. Primary progressive MS means disability worsens gradually from the start, without clear relapses.
[^noninferior]: Non-inferior means the new treatment worked about as well as the comparison drug, within a margin agreed beforehand. It does not mean it worked better.
[^mri]: Magnetic resonance imaging. A scan that shows the brain and spinal cord in detail. In MS it reveals lesions, which are areas of damage.
[^sels]: Slowly expanding lesions and paramagnetic rim lesions are lesions that keep smouldering at their edges instead of healing. They are thought to reflect ongoing low-level inflammation behind progression, so a drug that reduces them may be acting on progressive disease.
[^endpoints]: An endpoint is the measure a trial uses to decide whether a treatment worked, for example the time until disability worsens. "Did not reach statistical significance" means the difference seen could still be down to chance, so the benefit is not confirmed.
[^efficacy]: High-efficacy treatments are the ones that cut relapses and new lesions the most, for example ocrelizumab. Moderate-efficacy drugs reduce them less.
[^myelin]: Myelin is the insulating coating around nerve fibres. MS damages it and slows the signals. Remyelination is the repair of that coating.
[^oligo]: Oligodendrocytes are the brain cells that make and maintain myelin.
[^biomarker]: Something measurable, such as a blood test, scan or electrical test, that shows what is happening in the disease. Biomarkers let trials see an effect before changes in symptoms appear.
[^ebv]: Epstein-Barr virus. A very common virus that most adults carry, usually without symptoms. Almost everyone who develops MS has been infected, but most infected people never develop MS. That is what "required but not sufficient" means.
[^mono]: Infectious mononucleosis ("mono" or glandular fever) is an illness caused by EBV, often in teenagers. It shows up much sooner than MS, so a vaccine trial can see a result earlier.
[^aqp4]: AQP4 (aquaporin-4) is a water channel protein on astrocytes, the star-shaped support cells of the brain and spinal cord. In NMOSD, antibodies attack it.
[^phases]: Phase 1 trials test safety in a small group. Phase 2 looks for early signs that the treatment works. Phase 3 compares it with standard care in a large group and is usually needed for approval.
[^relapse]: Methylprednisolone is a high-dose steroid. Plasma exchange removes the liquid part of the blood, which carries the harmful antibodies, and replaces it. Complement inhibitors block a branch of the immune system, called complement, that causes damage in NMOSD.
[^il6]: Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a signalling protein that promotes inflammation. These drugs block IL-6 or its receptor.
[^gfapnfl]: NfL (neurofilament light chain) is a protein released into the blood when nerve cells are damaged. GFAP is released when astrocytes are damaged. Blood levels act as a gauge of recent damage.
[^conduction]: How quickly signals travel along the optic nerve, measured with a test called visual evoked potentials. Healthier myelin means faster signals.
[^metformin]: Metformin is a widely used diabetes medicine that is being tested for its effect on ageing stem cells in the brain.
[^seno]: Senotherapeutics are drugs that target senescent ("zombie") cells, which have stopped dividing but linger and release inflammatory signals.
[^bioage]: How old the body's tissues behave, estimated from blood markers or scans. It can differ from age in years.
[^comorbid]: Other health conditions that exist alongside MS.
[^hrt]: Hormone replacement therapy. Taking oestrogen, sometimes with progesterone, to ease menopause symptoms.
[^preclinical]: Done in lab dishes or animals, before any testing in people, so the results may not carry over.
