Parkinson's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterised by the loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra, a region of the midbrain that controls movement. As these neurons degenerate, dopamine levels fall, producing the disease's motor hallmarks: tremor, rigidity, slowness of movement (bradykinesia), and postural instability. Non-motor symptoms, including sleep disturbance, cognitive change, mood disorders, and loss of smell, often appear years before diagnosis.

The underlying pathology involves the abnormal accumulation of the protein alpha-synuclein into aggregates known as Lewy bodies. Most cases are sporadic, with age the strongest risk factor, though genetic and environmental contributions are well documented.

Current treatments, principally levodopa and dopamine agonists, manage symptoms by replacing or mimicking dopamine but do not stop the underlying neuronal loss. Research is now focused on disease-modifying and regenerative strategies: replacing lost neurons through stem-cell and cell-reprogramming approaches, protecting surviving neurons, targeting alpha-synuclein aggregation, and addressing the neuroinflammatory processes that contribute to progression.
